Moon

Tonight's Moon, drawn by the shadow that crosses it.

ItalianoEnglish

It opens and the Moon is already there, as it is right now: the lit part, the shadow crossing it, and the faint grey of the rest of the disc — the light the Earth throws back at it. One question, answered before you have finished asking it.

What it does

  • The phase in words and in numbers. "Waning crescent · 24% lit · 22.3 days old": whoever is looking out of the window wants the name, whoever is photographing wants the number.
  • The disc is drawn, not photographed. The terminator — the line between light and shadow — is the right curve, not half a circle: for all of the lunation bar two instants, that line is an ellipse.
  • Full and new, with the countdown. Date, time and days to go.
  • The next eclipses, with the kind spelled out — total, annular, partial or penumbral — and a note that the times are for the whole Earth and not for your balcony.
  • Drag through the lunation. The Moon waxes and wanes under your thumb, with a tap of haptics at every phase you cross. Double tap to come back to tonight.
  • In English and Italian, following the phone's language.

On the right minute, and off the network

The maths follows Jean Meeus's astronomical algorithms: the instants of new and full moon land on the right minute, and automated tests check it before every release. All of it happens on the phone — no location, no network, nothing to download — so Moon works up a mountain, on a plane and with mobile data off. Which is exactly where people look at the Moon.

Your data stays yours

There is nothing to send: no account, no sign-up, no server of ours, and not even your location. Two permissions — Internet, which the ads need, and vibration for the haptic feedback.

How it is paid for

A banner at the bottom, away from the controls, and an occasional full-screen ad only after you got your answer. No subscription, no feature held hostage.
